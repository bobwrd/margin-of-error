# 2026-08-06

India’s proposed return of merchant fees on some UPI payments is the clearest story today because it exposes the financing problem behind “free” digital infrastructure. The policy could preserve a widely used payments network, but it also tests whether digital inclusion survives when the cost is shifted from the state and payment firms to merchants. The distribution will depend on the threshold, the firms covered, and whether small sellers can pass the fee through without losing customers.

## 1. Development, justice, and access: a free payment rail still has a price

India’s Parliament is considering changes that could allow merchant discount rates on selected digital-payment transactions. The proposal under discussion would apply a fee of roughly 0.3% to 0.5% to UPI transactions above ₹2,000 for merchants with annual turnover above ₹15 million. The design matters: transactions above ₹2,000 are only about 4% of merchant payment volume but around 67% of value, so a high-value, large-merchant levy would raise funding while leaving most low-value payments untouched.

UPI is now too large to treat as a niche service. It processed 241.6 billion transactions worth ₹314.2 lakh crore in FY26, with 55.49 crore users recorded by June 2026. July alone reached 23.7 billion transactions. Those figures show why the payment rail has public-utility characteristics, even though it is run through banks and private payment applications. If transaction fees are introduced without clear cost allocation, the network may remain formally accessible while small merchants face higher charges, weaker bargaining power, or pressure to steer customers toward cash.

The access question is therefore not “fees or no fees.” It is who pays for resilience, fraud controls, interoperability, and settlement. A targeted fee on large merchants can be defensible if it funds the system and keeps person-to-person and small-ticket payments free. A broad fee would be less defensible because it would tax the very transactions through which low-income users and informal businesses gain from digital payments. The policy should publish the cost base, the expected revenue, and the share returned to merchants or consumers rather than treating “sustainable funding” as self-explanatory.

## 2. Everyday microeconomics: the incidence of a small percentage fee

A 0.3% fee sounds minor, but its effect depends on margins and payment alternatives. For a large retailer with high turnover and thin margins, the fee can become a material operating cost at scale. For a small shop, the direct amount may be low, but the merchant may have less ability to negotiate, reconcile charges, or tell whether a payment method is actually cheaper. A customer who sees no explicit surcharge may still pay indirectly through higher prices or fewer discounts.

This is a standard incidence problem disguised as a technical payment rule. The legal payer may be the merchant, but the economic burden can move to shoppers, workers, or suppliers. It can also change behaviour: merchants may prefer cash for some amounts, split transactions to stay below a threshold, or push customers toward a platform wallet. Those responses would make the headline fee rate a poor measure of the policy’s real effect.

The same logic applies to digital platforms more broadly. A platform can keep the consumer-facing price at zero while recovering costs from one side of a two-sided market. That can help adoption, but it also makes cross-subsidies difficult to see. Regulators should track not only the fee but also acceptance rates, failed transactions, merchant switching, and price changes in sectors where payment costs are easy to pass through.

## 3. Tech, AI, and the new political economy: platform power is moving into court

Two recent US cases show how platform disputes increasingly turn on access to information, distribution, and data. A judge allowed plaintiffs in an antitrust case to question LinkedIn’s current chief executive, bringing senior decision-makers into a dispute over the company’s market conduct. New Jersey also sued Amazon, alleging that its practices harmed competition in online retail; Amazon is already contesting antitrust cases brought by the Federal Trade Commission and California and denies the allegations.

These cases matter economically because platform power is rarely located in one posted price. It sits in ranking systems, seller terms, data access, switching costs, and the ability to decide which businesses reach customers. A platform can improve convenience while making participation harder to avoid. That is why remedies aimed only at a narrow fee may miss the source of market power. The stronger question is whether sellers and users have credible alternatives, can carry their data, and can reach customers without accepting one firm’s full set of conditions.

The data layer is also under pressure. A federal judge allowed Reddit to continue claims that Perplexity and other data scrapers bypassed protective measures to obtain content for AI training. The case does not settle whether all web scraping is unlawful, but it makes the economic conflict visible: platforms host user-created material, AI firms seek training inputs, and users may receive little of the value created from their contributions. The dispute is about technical access on the surface and surplus allocation underneath.

## 4. AI, labour, and regulation: productivity gains do not arrive evenly

Reuters reported that staffing at HDFC, ICICI, and other Indian banks has fallen by as much as 4% after years of expansion, as slower growth and automation reduce the need for some tasks. That is not evidence that AI alone caused the decline. It is evidence that automation becomes politically important when it meets a mature sector, weaker demand, and a labour market where entry-level jobs are part of the social bargain.

The relevant distinction is between task substitution and task complementarity. If software removes routine processing but increases demand for compliance, sales, risk review, or customer support, the gains can be shared through redeployment and training. If firms use automation mainly to reduce headcount while keeping the same output, productivity rises for the firm while the adjustment cost is carried by workers. The aggregate number can look positive even when a young worker loses the first rung into the industry.

The UK government’s position on advanced AI regulation shows the same tension at the policy level. Its AI minister said Britain would consider regulation if voluntary safeguards no longer protected the public. Voluntary rules can move quickly, but they place the burden of detecting harm on users, workers, and affected firms. A credible system needs measurable duties, access to remedies, and enough public capacity to test whether claimed safeguards work in practice. Otherwise “voluntary” becomes a way to delay the point at which firms must bear the cost of risk.

Across the week, the pattern is consistent. India is deciding who finances a public-facing digital rail; courts are testing who controls platform access and data; banks are showing how automation can change employment before productivity gains are broadly shared. In each case, the headline technology or legal rule is less revealing than the incidence: who absorbs the fee, who can wait for a remedy, who owns the input, and who has the power to exit.
