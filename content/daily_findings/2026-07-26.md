# 2026-07-26

The week’s clearest economic story was about control over information: who gets to use it, who pays when it is missing, and who can challenge decisions made with it. New Jersey moved against grocery “surveillance pricing,” North Carolina cut back a major source of civil legal aid, and regulators in the United Kingdom placed AI firms inside a supervised financial-testing environment. These are separate events, but they point to the same dispute over whether markets can be left to price, classify, and allocate access without stronger checks.

## 1. Development, justice, and access

North Carolina’s budget sharply reduced the future role of Interest on Lawyers’ Trust Accounts (IOLTA) in funding civil legal aid. The state’s IOLTA programme provided about $12 million for civil legal services in fiscal year 2025, including nearly $2 million received by Pisgah Legal Services. The new budget permits only $2 million a year from older IOLTA funds, with restrictions on which providers and cases can qualify. Most of the money is redirected toward criminal legal services, where indigent defendants already have a constitutional right to counsel. ([The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/newsletters/2026/07/iolta-north-carolina-legislature-funding-cut/688044/))

The economic effect is larger than the budget line suggests. Civil legal aid helps people contest evictions, recover disaster assistance, resist scams, secure benefits, and obtain protective orders. Without it, a low-income household may have a legal claim but no practical way to pursue it. The cost then shifts to housing instability, untreated disputes, emergency services, and uncompensated losses. A court system can remain formally open while becoming functionally pay-to-enter. The North Carolina case also shows how funding rules can shape the supply of representation before a judge ever considers the merits of a case.

## 2. Everyday microeconomics: pricing with personal data

New Jersey became the third US state this year to restrict personalised pricing based on personal or behavioural data. Its Fair Price Protection Act bars food retailers and grocery-delivery platforms from using information such as browsing history, location, income, household details, or other data to set different prices for individuals or groups. The law preserves ordinary loyalty discounts and does not ban every form of demand-based pricing. ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/new-jersey-bans-food-retailers-from-using-personalized-pricing))

The distinction matters. Conventional dynamic pricing responds to conditions such as inventory, time, or demand. Surveillance pricing tries to estimate a customer’s willingness to pay from information that the customer may not know is being used. That can turn a normal shopping decision into a hidden bargaining game in which the seller knows more about the buyer’s constraints than the buyer knows about the seller’s algorithm. The issue is not only whether the average price rises. It is whether households with fewer alternatives, less time, or more urgent needs are systematically charged more. The spread of similar laws across Maryland, Connecticut, and New Jersey suggests that data governance is becoming part of consumer-price regulation rather than a separate privacy issue.

## 3. Tech, finance, and regulatory capacity

The UK Financial Conduct Authority said Anthropic will support the second group of firms in its Supercharged Sandbox. Twenty-one organisations will test AI uses such as agent-led payments, fraud detection, governance, compliance, and services for vulnerable or underserved consumers. The second round received 199 applications, up from 132 in the first round, a 51% increase. The FCA also launched a 10-week Agentic Academy with the Centre for Finance, Technology and Entrepreneurship. ([FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/anthropic-supercharged-sandbox))

The economic question is who gets access to safe experimentation. A sandbox can reduce the cost of compliance for small firms and make it easier to test tools before they reach consumers. But it can also create a privileged channel where a major AI provider helps define the practical limits of acceptable use. The FCA says it will rely on existing frameworks rather than add AI-specific regulation. That approach may reduce delay, but it places more weight on supervisory skill, audit access, and the ability of regulators to detect harms after deployment. For financial inclusion, the test is concrete: an AI service should widen access without turning thin credit histories, disability, language, or financial distress into new reasons for exclusion.

Anthropic also announced another $20 million donation to Public First Action, bringing its total support for the organisation to $40 million. Anthropic says the money is for public education and policy work, not candidate election activity, and argues for stronger testing, transparency, independent evaluation, and enforcement powers for frontier AI developers. ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/donation-public-first-action))

This is a political-economy event as much as a corporate donation. Firms that may be regulated are now financing parts of the debate about how they should be regulated. That does not make the policy proposals wrong, but it changes the balance of expertise and access around lawmakers. The public-interest question is whether the rules are written around measurable duties—testing, disclosure, auditability, liability—or around the preferred policy language of the firms with the most resources to participate.

## 4. AI and labour: productivity claims meet legal proof

A lawsuit by Meta workers alleges that AI-based tools used in layoff selection relied on productivity and AI-token measures that disadvantaged workers who had taken medical or family leave. A US judge declined to block the layoffs while the claims proceed, and the workers face private arbitration rather than a public class action. Reuters reported that the plaintiffs’ lawyers have struggled to obtain evidence showing how the systems were used. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/world-at-work/meta-employees-lawsuit-shows-that-if-ai-fires-you-proving-it-is-hard-part-2026-07-22/))

The mechanism is familiar from older workplace disputes, but the evidentiary problem is newer. An employer can describe a model as a productivity tool while withholding the training data, feature definitions, thresholds, and human overrides needed to test that description. Arbitration then limits collective discovery and public scrutiny. If a worker must prove both discrimination and the hidden operation of a system controlled by the employer, the legal right may exist on paper but remain expensive to exercise.

The OECD’s 2026 Employment Outlook adds a wider distributional frame. Employment-rate differences between regions exceed 20 percentage points in more than half of OECD economies. The report says the direct household impact of the 2022 energy shock was at least 30% higher in rural areas than in urban centres where comparable data are available, and estimates that shrinking workforces could raise labour shortages by 15% within the next 15 years in the 20% of regions with the highest old-age dependency ratios. ([OECD Employment Outlook 2026](https://doi.org/10.1787/7e710f54-en))

Those figures complicate simple claims that AI productivity will lift everyone. The gains will depend on where firms, data centres, skills, and high-speed connectivity are located, as well as who can move into the new jobs. A national average can improve while particular regions lose bargaining power and income. Policy therefore has to track the distribution of productivity gains, not only the amount of output produced.

The week’s pattern is a contest over hidden allocation systems. A grocery algorithm can decide what a household pays, a budget decision can decide whether a poor person can use the courts, and an employment model can decide who bears the cost of restructuring. In each case, the formal market or institution remains in place, but access depends on information, money, and the ability to challenge the decision. The most useful response is not to reject technology or markets outright. It is to require visible rules, usable appeal routes, and enough public capacity to test whether the promised gains reach the people who carry the risk.
