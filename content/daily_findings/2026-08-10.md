# 2026-08-10

The week’s most consequential economic story is shifting from whether AI investment will continue to who controls the infrastructure and can correct the decisions made by AI systems. Australia is now treating interactions among autonomous agents as a governance problem, while Indonesia is planning a 1GW AI factory and nationwide 5G coverage by 2029. These projects may widen access to useful tools, but they also create new dependence on electricity, networks, data, and firms able to operate them.

## 1. Finance and macroeconomics: AI investment is carrying more of the growth story

Deloitte’s weekly economic update reports that US real GDP grew at an annualized 1.5% in the second quarter, down from 2.1% in the first. Consumer spending rose 3.2%, but the personal saving rate fell from 3.5% in March to 2.7% in June. Business investment remained strong: real nonresidential fixed investment rose 8.4%, with equipment investment up 15.2% and intellectual-property investment up 8.8%. Information-technology equipment and software accounted for 46% of second-quarter GDP growth, according to the analysis.

That mix is productive but fragile. Spending supported by a falling saving rate cannot keep accelerating indefinitely, while AI-related investment must eventually produce enough revenue or cost reduction to justify the capital. The Bank for International Settlements has warned that heavy leverage and circular financing across the AI supply chain can make a boom more vulnerable to excess capacity. The distributional question arrives before any correction: if firms invest in machines and software while hiring slows, output can rise without a matching improvement in entry-level jobs or wages.

## 2. Development and access: infrastructure is necessary, but access is not the same as ownership

Indonesia has set a target of nationwide 5G coverage by 2029 and is moving ahead with Zankore, a planned AI Factory expected to reach 1GW of computing capacity within three years. Construction is scheduled to begin in the first half of 2027 in Batang, Central Java. The project is also tied to Sahabat-AI, an open-source model for Bahasa Indonesia and regional languages including Javanese, Sundanese, Balinese, and Batak. A local-language model and lower-cost access to computing could help public agencies, small firms, and researchers who are poorly served by imported systems.

The economic test is whether the infrastructure broadens productive capacity or mainly subsidizes a new concentration of power. A 1GW facility needs reliable electricity, water, land, skilled workers, and long-term demand. If those inputs are publicly supported while access is priced through a small number of cloud and telecom firms, the public may bear the cost without receiving a fair share of the surplus. Indonesia’s 2029 5G target raises the same issue at the network layer: coverage statistics matter less than affordability, service quality, local-language support, and a usable route to challenge an automated decision.

## 3. Justice and access: digital grievance systems reduce filing costs, not the need for remedy

India’s Centralised Public Grievance Redress and Monitoring System is being expanded with multilingual services, data analytics, AI-supported routing, and a voice chatbot called “Samadhan Didi.” The government says annual grievance disposals rose from about 301,000 in 2014 to around 2.7 million in 2024, while grievance redress officers increased from 10,232 to more than 111,000 by 2025. The system also includes appeal mechanisms for unresolved cases, with appellate review expected within 30 days.

This is a useful model of administrative access because voice input and language support can remove barriers that online forms impose. But CPGRAMS excludes court proceedings, so it is not a substitute for legal aid or an accessible court system. Its success should be judged by whether complaints reach the correct authority, whether officials give reasons, how often decisions are appealed or reversed, and whether people without stable connectivity can use the service. AI can lower the cost of entering a complaint; it cannot by itself equalise the bargaining power between a citizen and the state agency that decides the complaint.

## 4. Tech, AI, and the new political economy: control is moving to the system around the model

Australia’s AI Safety Institute published its first report on risks created when AI agents interact across organisations, suppliers, customers, and the open internet. The problem is different from a single model producing a bad answer. Multiple agents may pass instructions, data, or permissions across institutional boundaries, leaving no single organisation with a complete view of the chain. The proposed response focuses on mapping risks to the parties able to intervene, identifying gaps where no actor has enough authority or technical capacity to respond.

Singapore’s Ministry of Digital Development and Information has likewise reaffirmed human and organisational accountability for agentic AI, while continuing to assess whether AI-generated content should eventually require mandatory labelling. The contrast is useful. Australia is stressing shared controls for interconnected systems; Singapore is stressing designated oversight and risk-based review. Neither approach resolves the underlying political-economy problem: firms and governments may deploy agents faster than workers, consumers, or affected citizens can inspect their actions. The value of transparency lies in giving someone enough information and authority to stop, contest, or reverse a harmful outcome.

The distribution problem also appears in the AI supply chain. Vietnam is being advised to begin with semiconductor manufacturing and packaging, then build capacity in equipment, materials, design, and testing. That is a more realistic path than trying to reproduce the entire value chain at once, but it also shows where surplus is captured. Assembly and packaging can create jobs, while control over advanced equipment, intellectual property, and design standards captures more of the value. Vietnam’s policy choice, like Indonesia’s AI Factory, will be judged by skills, domestic supplier development, and bargaining power rather than by the headline size of the project.

Across the week, macroeconomic data, digital public services, platform disputes, and AI infrastructure pointed to the same pattern. New systems are lowering some visible costs while moving hidden costs into savings, privacy, legal knowledge, energy, and control over data. The practical question is incidence: who owns the infrastructure, who receives the productivity gain, who can appeal a decision, and who pays when the system fails.
