# 2026-08-09

This week’s clearest economic story is a distribution problem disguised as a technology and market story. AI is being presented as a route to faster development and higher productivity, while courts and regulators are deciding who controls the data, infrastructure, and rules that determine whether those gains reach ordinary users. At the same time, weaker US hiring and higher legal uncertainty show that adjustment costs arrive before promised benefits.

## 1. Finance and macroeconomics: weak hiring meets expensive adjustment

The US July employment report gave markets a weaker labour signal than the 4.1% unemployment rate suggests. Payroll employment fell by 23,000, May and June gains were revised down by a combined 103,000, and labour-force participation was 61.4%, down 0.7 percentage points since January. Financial activities employment fell by 14,000 in July and was down 121,000 from its May 2025 peak. The combination matters because a stable unemployment rate can coexist with fewer people entering or remaining in the measured labour market.

This changes the distributional reading of monetary policy. A household with savings can wait for inflation to ease or rates to fall. A household with irregular income may face a higher cost of credit, weaker hiring prospects, and less ability to absorb a rent, health, or food shock. Mexico’s central bank held its policy rate at 6.5% and pushed back the expected return of inflation to its 3% target until late 2027, showing the same tension: restraint may be justified at the macro level, but the burden is heavier for borrowers without a buffer.

The US tariff litigation adds a second adjustment cost. The administration has reportedly refunded about $100 billion in tariffs collected before the Supreme Court struck the duties down. A court ruling can reverse the legal basis for a charge, but it does not instantly reverse the economic decisions made around it. Importers may have already raised prices, changed suppliers, or delayed investment. The refund process also favours firms with the records and legal capacity to prove what they paid, which makes remedy a question of administrative access as well as doctrine.

## 2. Development and access: financial inclusion requires infrastructure and protection

The World Bank’s World Development Report 2026 argues that AI could help developing countries make rapid gains in health, education, agriculture, tax administration, and judicial services. Its employment estimates sharpen the point: 4.5% of existing jobs in low- and middle-income countries are at risk of automation by generative AI, compared with 14.2% in high-income countries, while 16.2% of jobs in developing economies could receive a meaningful productivity boost. The report’s central condition is basic infrastructure. Nearly one-third of rural schools in Sub-Saharan Africa still lack reliable electricity, and more than two-thirds lack dependable internet access.

The numbers suggest that the first economic divide is not access to a frontier model. It is whether a person has electricity, connectivity, local-language data, and an institution that can correct an error. A small AI tool that helps a nurse or agricultural extension worker can complement scarce expertise. An automated credit or benefits decision without a usable appeal channel can make exclusion harder to see and harder to reverse. In this setting, “digital inclusion” is not a count of registered accounts. It is the ability to use a service at reasonable cost and obtain a human remedy when the system is wrong.

Recent evidence on SAARC economies points in the same direction. A 2000–2024 panel study found that digital financial innovation was associated with better long-run sustainable-development outcomes, while credit-led inclusion without responsible-lending safeguards could produce negative effects. That distinction matters for digital credit. A loan app can reduce travel and paperwork, but it can also price risk through opaque data, renew debt at high cost, or use aggressive collection practices. The relevant policy measures are repeat borrowing, total repayment, complaint resolution, and the share of borrowers who remain financially better off after the loan, not the number of disbursements.

## 3. Justice and everyday markets: data access and procedure decide who benefits

A US appeals court ruled that a New York City law requiring food-delivery companies to share customer data with restaurants was unconstitutional. The case sits at the intersection of privacy, platform power, and bargaining. Restaurants argue that they need customer information to reduce dependence on delivery intermediaries. Platforms treat the same information as an asset that supports pricing, retention, and control over the customer relationship. Consumers are not simply choosing between privacy and competition: the legal allocation of data determines which firms can build a direct relationship and which remain dependent on a platform.

The access problem is practical. If restaurants receive more data but customers receive no clearer notice or control, a pro-competition measure can still expand surveillance. If platforms retain all data, restaurants may face high commissions and limited ability to move customers elsewhere. A better rule would separate the questions: what information is necessary for order fulfilment, what information is needed for legitimate competition, and what information should require explicit consent. That is a more useful test than treating “data sharing” as either automatically pro-consumer or automatically harmful.

The US Department of Justice also rescinded longstanding guidance affecting proxy advisers, citing antitrust concerns about consolidation in the investor-advice industry. Proxy advisers help institutional investors evaluate corporate votes, so concentration can affect how many shareholders receive similar recommendations from a small number of information providers. The broader lesson is that market power can arise in an information layer that looks secondary to the underlying transaction. When investors, merchants, or consumers depend on a narrow set of interpreters, access to the market may remain formally open while practical choice narrows.

## 4. Tech, AI, and labour: productivity depends on who owns the inputs

The World Bank’s AI report is more useful than a simple automation forecast because it distinguishes task replacement from task support. Developing economies may face less immediate displacement because fewer jobs are exposed to generative AI, yet they may gain from tools that extend the reach of doctors, teachers, judges, and public officials. The risk is that the productivity gain is captured by the organisation controlling the software, data, and procurement contract rather than by the worker whose task becomes more effective.

That allocation problem is already visible in the legal disputes around AI training data and model deployment. Authors, software developers, and other rights holders are asking whether their work can be used as a model input without payment or meaningful control. Companies argue over discovery, responsibility, and the knowledge held by senior executives. The economic issue is bargaining power: if a platform can copy or aggregate an input at low marginal cost, the input supplier may receive little of the value created from it unless law or collective action changes the negotiating position.

AI regulation therefore has to cover more than model behaviour. It also needs rules for data provenance, worker monitoring, automated decisions, procurement, and access to appeal. The World Bank warns that advanced systems are concentrated in a small number of countries and firms. Without local data, public capacity, and open enough markets for adaptation, developing economies may become users of imported systems while paying recurring fees and accepting decisions they cannot inspect. That is a dependency problem, not merely a software problem.

Across the week, the same pattern appeared in finance, justice, and technology. Systems are becoming faster and more connected, but the gains depend on who can afford to wait, understand the rules, challenge a decision, or claim ownership of the input. Weak hiring makes the cost of adjustment visible in advanced economies; digital credit and AI infrastructure make it visible in developing ones. The useful test is incidence: who pays, who receives the productivity gain, who controls the data, and who has a practical route to appeal.
