# 2026-08-03

The week’s most useful signal is that digital systems are becoming part of the ordinary cost of living before their benefits are evenly shared. India’s central bank is weighing whether to hold rates while payments run at record scale; regulators in New Jersey, the United States, and the European Union are trying to stop algorithms and interfaces from turning information gaps into higher prices, recurring charges, or unreviewable decisions.

## 1. Finance and monetary policy: payment scale is growing faster than protection

The Reserve Bank of India’s Monetary Policy Committee began its three-day meeting on 3 August, with markets expecting the repo rate to remain unchanged. The policy decision is due on 5 August. The hold reflects a familiar trade-off: growth still needs credit, but a weaker rupee, imported energy costs, and uncertainty in global markets can return through inflation. A wait-and-see stance also gives the RBI time to observe whether price pressure is broad-based rather than concentrated in a few goods.

India’s payments data shows why monetary and access questions cannot be separated. UPI transactions rose 4.1% in July to 23.66 billion, according to reporting on NPCI data. A low-cost instant payment rail reduces the need for cash and can help small firms receive money quickly. But transaction volume does not tell us whether households have safe credit, bargaining power, or a remedy when a payment is fraudulent. A person can be fully connected to the payment system and still be excluded from affordable borrowing or from recovering a small disputed loss.

The same distributional issue appears in the wider AI investment cycle. MAS has warned that AI-related firms account for about 40% of S&P 500 market capitalisation and 87% of new US venture-capital funding. If the investment produces broad productivity gains, output and incomes may rise. If expected revenue fails to catch up with infrastructure spending, the adjustment will reach chip suppliers, data-centre contractors, lenders, and workers whose local economies depend on the build-out. The risk is not simply that technology shares fall; it is that the financing structure concentrates the upside while spreading the downside through credit and employment.

## 2. Development, justice, and access: a legal right is weak when the remedy costs more than the loss

Nigeria’s Federal High Court has cleared the way for enforcement of the Digital, Electronic, Online or Non-Traditional Consumer Lending Regulations after dismissing the challenge brought by the Wireless Application Service Providers Association of Nigeria. The regulations cover digital lenders and related services, including mobile lending and buy-now-pay-later products. They require registration, licensing, consumer disclosures, and controls around data use, although an appeal remains possible.

The economic issue is not only whether a lender is licensed. Small-dollar borrowers often accept a loan because the need is immediate and comparison is costly. If the lender controls the interface, repayment reminders, contact permissions, and transaction records, the borrower must later prove that a charge, disclosure, or collection practice was improper. A disclosure rule helps only when the terms are readable, the borrower can keep a record, and an affordable complaint route exists. Otherwise the law gives formal consent more weight than practical bargaining power.

Singapore’s Money Lock programme points to a different type of intervention. MAS said about S$47 billion was protected from digital transfers through Money Lock as of May 2026, nearly twice the amount a year earlier, while scam cases and losses fell in 2025 for the first time since 2021. The mechanism changes the default before a loss occurs. That is valuable, but it does not address people who accept expensive informal credit because they cannot wait for a safer option. Inclusion should therefore be judged by the quality of the choices available under pressure, not by account ownership alone.

## 3. Everyday microeconomics: algorithms and subscriptions can convert limited choice into extra payment

New Jersey signed the Forbidding the Algorithmic Inflation of Rent Act on 20 July. The law targets rent-setting tools that use non-public information from competing landlords, such as occupancy or lease data, to recommend prices. The concern is that a shared algorithm can reduce independent pricing decisions even when landlords never directly agree on a rent. In a housing market with few vacant units, that matters because tenants cannot easily respond by switching suppliers.

The case also shows why algorithmic pricing is different from an ordinary price increase. A tenant may see only the final rent, not the data pool, model assumptions, or number of competing properties that shaped it. If several landlords use the same service, the software can make a coordinated high price look like a series of independent business decisions. New Jersey’s approach treats the data and the pricing process as part of the competition problem, rather than waiting for consumers to prove that an individual landlord lied.

The FTC’s lawsuit against Hims & Hers illustrates the household version of the same problem. The agency alleges that the company charged consumers for prescriptions shortly after they submitted intake forms, enrolled many in recurring plans without a meaningful chance to approve the treatment, and made cancellation difficult. These are allegations, not a final judgment. If proved, the design would use status quo bias and the consumer’s sunk time in the intake process to keep payments flowing. Requiring the first total charge and refill date to be clear, with cancellation as easy as sign-up, would address the incentive more directly than another long disclosure.

## 4. Tech, AI, and the new political economy: governance is arriving before the productivity settlement

The European Union’s AI Act entered a new enforcement phase on 2 August. The European AI Office and national authorities can supervise covered systems, request technical documentation, evaluate general-purpose models, require corrective measures, and issue fines for non-compliance. Transparency duties now apply in the covered cases, while high-risk areas include employment, credit, access to essential services, and parts of the administration of justice. The practical test is whether the person affected can identify that AI was used, understand who is responsible, and challenge an error without needing to hire a specialist.

The EU is pursuing industrial policy at the same time. Its call for up to seven AI gigafactories could mobilise more than €30 billion in investment and expand access to advanced computing. That may reduce dependence on a small number of cloud and chip providers, but public funding does not automatically create a competitive market. If smaller firms, universities, public agencies, and auditors cannot access the compute on workable terms, the state may finance infrastructure that strengthens the largest users instead of widening participation.

This is also a labour question. AI productivity is often measured as output per hour inside the adopting firm, but the distribution depends on what happens to tasks, wages, and worker control. A system that drafts routine work may complement a skilled employee; one that ranks applicants or sets schedules may shift risk onto workers while leaving the firm with the measurable gain. Regulation that requires logs, oversight, and documentation can improve accountability, but it does not by itself decide who receives the surplus from automation.

Across the week, finance, consumer law, and AI governance pointed to one pattern: digital systems lower transaction costs for the firm that controls the payment rail, interface, data, or infrastructure, while people with less time and money carry the uncertainty. The policy question is concrete: can a person see the rule, stop the charge or decision, and obtain a remedy at a cost below the loss? When the answer is no, high payment volume can coexist with unsafe credit, algorithmic concentration, and productivity gains that remain narrowly held.
