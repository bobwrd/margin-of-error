# 2026-08-08

This week’s most revealing economic signal is not a market index but a distributional split: the US added almost no payroll jobs in July, while investors continued to price large gains from AI infrastructure and productivity. Across payments, courts, consumer subscriptions, and AI copyright, the same question keeps returning: who receives the benefit, and who is left carrying the adjustment cost?

## 1. Finance and work: a weak hiring signal beneath a stable unemployment rate

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payroll employment fell by 23,000 in July, after an average monthly gain of 34,000 over the previous year. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%, but the labour-force participation rate slipped to 61.4%, down 0.7 percentage points since January. May and June payroll growth was also revised down by a combined 103,000. A stable unemployment rate therefore does not mean that the labour market is stable: fewer people are entering or remaining in the measured labour force, and the job count is being revised toward a weaker picture.

The sector detail matters for household income. Financial-activities employment fell by 14,000 in July and is down 121,000 from its May 2025 peak. Retail lost 19,000 jobs, while health care added 22,000, below its prior twelve-month average. Average hourly earnings rose 3.2% over the year, but that aggregate does not show who can find full-time work, who has lost hours, or who has stopped searching. The policy risk is that a headline unemployment rate encourages premature confidence while weaker hiring makes it harder for new workers, migrants, and people with limited savings to recover from a shock.

The report also complicates the productivity story. If firms invest heavily in software and AI while hiring slows, the gain may show up first as lower labour demand rather than higher pay. That does not prove that AI caused July’s job losses. It does mean that productivity claims should be tested against entry-level hiring, hours, wage growth by occupation, and the share of workers moving into better-paid tasks.

## 2. Development and access: India is deciding who pays for a public payment rail

India’s debate over bringing back merchant discount rates on some UPI transactions is moving from a technical payments question into a distribution question. The proposed legal change would remove UPI’s automatic statutory exemption from merchant charges, while reports indicate that any future fee could focus on larger merchants and higher-value transactions. UPI processed a record 23.66 billion transactions worth ₹29.88 lakh crore in July 2026, according to reporting based on NPCI data. At that scale, the network has some characteristics of public infrastructure even though banks and private payment apps handle much of the user interface.

A merchant fee can help fund the banks and payment providers that keep the system running. But the legal payer and the economic payer may differ. A large retailer may absorb the charge, a small business may raise prices or steer customers toward cash, and a platform may use the fee to favour its own wallet or credit product. Exempting small merchants and low-value payments would limit direct harm, but it would not answer whether costs are shifted through prices or whether the system becomes more concentrated around high-value transactions.

The wider inclusion test is stricter than counting accounts or payment volume. A financial service is useful when it remains affordable, reliable, and contestable after a dispute or failed transaction. Policymakers should publish the fee’s incidence, complaint rates, merchant exit, failed-payment rates, and the effect on users who rely on digital payments because cash access is costly. Otherwise a service can remain free for consumers while becoming less accessible at the point where low-income merchants earn their income.

## 3. Justice and law: digitisation can reduce travel costs, but representation still decides outcomes

India’s Press Information Bureau reported that the e-Courts project has digitised more than 753 crore pages, processed over 1.25 crore cases through e-filing, and supported more than 4.18 crore remote hearings. It also reported 1,806 functional e-Sewa Kendras in court complexes, with these centres helping litigants check case status, file documents, make payments, and find legal-aid services. These are real reductions in time and travel costs, especially for people living far from a court or unable to pay repeated administrative expenses.

The access question is whether digital systems reduce the advantage held by parties who can pay for lawyers and procedural expertise. Online filing does not explain a legal document, prepare evidence, or protect an accused person from an error in a form. India’s own figures show why assistance remains necessary: the project records about 35 lakh daily hits on the multilingual e-Courts portal, while e-Sewa Kendras are needed to help people use the same systems. Technology lowers some transaction costs, but it can also move the burden from clerks to litigants who lack stable internet access, language support, or legal knowledge.

The US immigration system shows the same issue in a more acute form. A federal contract for legal services for unaccompanied immigrant children lapsed on July 31, affecting the Acacia Center for Justice and nearly 100 subcontractors. One subcontractor reported 243 active cases while using up its savings and stopping new cases. The proposed replacement contract is worth up to $150 million, but the disruption came while more than three million immigration cases remained in the system. A right to counsel that depends on unstable funding is a formal right with an unequal economic value.

## 4. Everyday microeconomics: subscriptions and privacy turn inertia into revenue

The Federal Trade Commission and state partners sued Hims & Hers on July 29, alleging that the telehealth provider charged many users after they submitted an intake form, enrolled them in recurring treatment plans, made cancellation difficult, and shared sensitive health information with advertising platforms. The complaint alleges that the cancellation button was hidden behind several navigation steps and that users were not clearly told when prescriptions would be refilled. These allegations remain for the court to decide, but the economic mechanism is familiar: a small amount of friction at sign-up and cancellation changes behaviour at scale.

This is status quo bias with a billing system attached. A consumer may postpone cancellation because the cost is not immediately visible, while recurring revenue continues automatically. Sensitive data adds another layer: the user is asked to exchange information in a setting where privacy matters, but the surplus from that data can be captured by the provider and advertising platforms. Consumer protection therefore has to cover the whole transaction, including the timing of charges, the path to exit, and the downstream use of data. A disclosure buried in a privacy policy does little when the practical choice architecture points in one direction.

The same reasoning applies to dynamic pricing and algorithmic competition. Shared pricing software can make rival firms’ rates easier to coordinate even when no manager sends a direct instruction. The Third Circuit’s revival of claims involving hotel pricing software, reported in legal analysis this week, keeps attention on whether software reduces search costs for consumers or reduces uncertainty for sellers. The distinction is not academic: one mechanism can produce lower prices, while the other can make a market look competitive while weakening price competition.

## 5. Tech and the new political economy: AI investment is becoming a dispute over ownership and bargaining power

Meta asked a California federal judge to block a deposition of Mark Zuckerberg in four copyright lawsuits concerning the company’s AI training practices. Meta argues that the plaintiffs have not shown that Zuckerberg has unique firsthand knowledge and have not exhausted other discovery options. The procedural dispute points to a larger economic conflict over training data. Authors and other rights holders are asking whether their work is an input that can be copied into a model without payment, while platforms argue over who within a large company can be required to explain the choices behind that use.

The bargaining problem is not limited to copyright. AI firms need data, computing capacity, distribution, and workers who label, verify, moderate, and integrate model outputs. The firms that control the model and the distribution channel can capture a larger share of the resulting surplus than the people who supply those inputs. A legal rule requiring traceable training sources, meaningful opt-outs, or compensation would change that bargaining position. A rule that only asks companies to publish broad principles may leave the underlying allocation unchanged.

US lawmakers also proposed an AI tax this week to fund a jobs programme aimed at workers affected by automation, according to Politico. The proposal is early-stage, and its effect would depend on how the tax defines AI revenue, prevents avoidance, and distributes the funds. Still, it addresses the missing link in many productivity debates: investment gains do not automatically become worker gains. A credible policy would measure displacement, job quality, training completion, and wage progression rather than treating aggregate output as proof that workers benefited.

Across the week, payment rails, courts, labour markets, subscriptions, and AI systems showed the same pattern. Institutions are reducing visible friction for some users while shifting hidden costs onto people with less money, time, data, or legal power. The most useful economic question is incidence: who pays, who can appeal, who owns the input, and who has a practical way to leave.
