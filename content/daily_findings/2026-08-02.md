# 2026-08-02

The week’s clearest economic signal came from the collision between the AI investment boom and the institutions meant to contain its risks. Singapore’s central bank warned that AI-related firms now dominate large parts of equity and new-financing markets, while the European Union began enforcing transparency rules that affect how people encounter AI in finance, employment, public services, and legal settings. The same question sits underneath both developments: who receives the gains, and who pays when the system is wrong?

## 1. Finance and monetary policy: the AI build-out is now a macroeconomic exposure

The Monetary Authority of Singapore said AI-related firms account for about 40% of S&P 500 market capitalisation and more than 30% of the MSCI Emerging Markets Asia index. They also represent roughly half of new US investment-grade bond issuance, 38% of high-yield issuance, and 87% of new venture-capital funding. MAS warned that projected spending by hyperscalers and model builders has moved beyond current commercial revenue, meaning the next stage will depend on large amounts of equity and debt financing.

That creates a two-sided macro risk. If productivity gains spread across firms, higher output may support income, demand, and eventually inflation. If expected returns disappoint, the adjustment would not stay inside technology stocks. Data-centre construction, chip orders, supplier finance, corporate credit, and household wealth would all feel the pullback. MAS’s stress tests found Singapore’s financial system broadly resilient, but identified thinner buffers among highly leveraged households and corporates. The distribution matters because a large company can delay investment, refinance, or hedge; a household with unstable income often cannot.

US data pointed in the same direction. Equipment spending rose at a 15.2% annual rate in the second quarter, helped by AI investment, but imports subtracted 1.01 percentage points from GDP growth as the build-out relied heavily on foreign hardware. The Federal Reserve held rates steady on 29 July while inflation remained above target, and the 30-year Treasury yield later reached 5.2%, its highest level in 19 years. Long-term borrowing costs can therefore tighten conditions for housing and smaller firms even when the policy rate does not move.

## 2. Development, justice, and access: legal protection depends on the cost of using it

A Federal High Court in Lagos dismissed WASPAN’s challenge to Nigeria’s Digital, Electronic, Online or Non-Traditional Consumer Lending Regulations and discharged the interim order that had paused enforcement. The rules cover digital lenders, mobile lending apps, and buy-now-pay-later services. They require registration, licensing, consumer disclosures, and controls around data use. The decision also kept a boundary between agencies: the FCCPC can enforce consumer and competition rules, while telecom licensing remains with the NCC. WASPAN has indicated that it will appeal, so enforcement clarity may remain contested even though the regulations currently stand.

The case matters beyond compliance. Small-dollar digital loans are often used when a borrower has no time to compare terms or negotiate. A rule requiring disclosure is useful only if the borrower can understand the price, challenge abusive collection, and obtain a remedy without spending more than the disputed amount. If the platform holds the records, changes the interface, and contacts the borrower through a phone that is also needed for work, the legal relationship is formally equal but practically unequal.

Singapore’s anti-scam measures show a different approach to access. MAS said about S$47 billion was protected from digital transfers through Money Lock as of May 2026, nearly twice the amount a year earlier, while scam cases and losses fell in 2025 for the first time since 2021. This works by changing the default path before a loss occurs. It does not solve every access problem. People with low digital confidence, unstable income, or limited alternatives may still accept expensive credit or unsafe payment arrangements because immediate liquidity matters more than a future remedy.

## 3. Everyday microeconomics: small interface choices can shift the burden onto consumers

The US Federal Trade Commission and state officials sued Hims & Hers over alleged privacy, billing, and cancellation practices. The complaint alleges that consumers could be enrolled in recurring prescriptions and charged after completing an intake flow without a clear understanding that they were entering a subscription. It also alleges that sensitive health information was shared with advertising platforms and that cancellation was made difficult. These are allegations, not final findings, but the case shows how consumer harm can arise from a sequence of low-friction decisions rather than one visible fee.

The economics are familiar. The seller controls the interface and payment records, while the buyer must notice the charge, identify the legal issue, preserve evidence, contact the company, and escalate if needed. Status quo bias then keeps the subscription active. A cancellation rule that mirrors sign-up, a plain statement of the first total charge, and a clear treatment date would reduce the gap between formal consent and informed consent more directly than another long disclosure.

Platform pricing raises a related problem. A July survey reported by the Computer & Communications Industry Association found that about 70% of UK adults considered payment and app-security checks very important, while roughly two-thirds worried that more purchases outside app stores could create negative outcomes. The survey was commissioned by a platform-industry group, so it should not settle the competition debate. It does show the trade-off regulators face: app stores can use their control over payments to fund fraud checks and security, but the same control can support high fees and restrict rival payment routes. The policy question is whether safety functions can be funded without allowing the store to set the terms of the whole market.

## 4. Tech, AI, and the new political economy: governance is arriving before the productivity payoff is settled

The European Commission says the AI Act’s transparency rules and enforcement arrangements begin applying on 2 August 2026. People must be told when they interact with certain AI systems, and AI-generated content must be identifiable in the covered cases. The Commission’s examples of high-risk uses include recruitment, worker management, credit scoring, access to essential services, law enforcement, and the administration of justice. Strict obligations for many high-risk systems, including risk assessment, logging, documentation, human oversight, and controls against discriminatory outcomes, now begin on 2 December 2027, with some product-related systems extended to August 2028.

The delay creates a practical tension. It gives providers more time to document systems and build testing capacity, but workers and applicants may face automated evaluation before the full duties apply. In recruitment or credit, the person affected often cannot see the training data, the variables used, or the error rate. Transparency at the point of interaction is a start; it does not by itself provide a right to correct the record or a low-cost path to appeal. The value of AI productivity therefore depends partly on whether institutions can turn an automated decision into an accountable one.

The EU also launched a call for seven AI gigafactories that could mobilise more than €30 billion in investment. This is an attempt to widen access to computing capacity, but public funding does not automatically produce a competitive market. If the infrastructure is usable only by the largest firms, the state may subsidise concentration rather than reduce it. A better test is whether smaller firms, universities, public agencies, and workers can access compute, data, and technical expertise on terms that let them build or audit systems without becoming dependent on a single provider.

Across finance, consumer markets, and AI regulation, the week showed the same pattern. Digital systems reduce transaction costs for the firms that control the data, interface, and infrastructure, but they often push uncertainty onto people with less time, money, or bargaining power. The practical measure of inclusion is simple: can a person understand the rule, stop the charge or decision, and obtain a remedy at a cost below the loss? Where the answer is no, formal access can coexist with weak competition, expensive credit, and productivity gains that remain concentrated.
