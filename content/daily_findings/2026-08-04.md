# 2026-08-04

The most consequential event today is Indonesia’s abrupt change at the top of Bank Indonesia. A central bank that markets have treated as an autonomous stabiliser now faces a leadership transition widely read through the lens of presidential pressure, while the rupiah has already fallen 14% against the dollar since President Prabowo took office. Around that political-economy story, regulators are trying to contain two related forms of private power: pricing systems that use personal data to charge different consumers, and AI systems whose errors can affect work, credit, and access to services.

## 1. Development, justice, and access: safe participation depends on bargaining power

Bangladesh has launched a National Action Plan for 2026–2030 to implement the Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration. The plan matters because migration is both a household strategy and a macroeconomic stabiliser: annual remittance inflows have exceeded US$30 billion in recent years, supporting consumption, education, housing, and foreign-currency availability. But the route to that income is often financed through land sales, mortgages, and high-interest borrowing. When recruitment agents overcharge, substitute contracts, or route workers through traffickers, the initial cost of “inclusion” becomes a debt claim against the whole household.

The useful test is not whether a worker can leave through a formal channel on paper. It is whether the worker can verify the employer, understand the contract, report abuse, and obtain help in a destination country without spending more than the claim is worth. Digital recruitment records and verified contracts can reduce information gaps, but they do not replace enforcement. Bilateral labour agreements need remedies for wage theft, passport confiscation, contract substitution, and unsafe conditions. Otherwise the worker bears the downside while recruiters and employers capture the economic surplus.

A second access question is visible in India’s monetary-policy meeting, which began on 3 August ahead of the Reserve Bank of India’s decision on 5 August. Rate policy is usually discussed through inflation and growth, but the distribution runs through borrowers’ balance sheets. A small firm or migrant household facing an urgent cash need cannot hedge a rate shock or wait for cheaper formal credit. This is why financial inclusion should cover the price, reliability, and legal safety of credit, not only the existence of a bank account or instant-payment ID.

## 2. Finance and monetary institutions: credibility is an economic asset

Bank Indonesia Governor Perry Warjiyo resigned last week after, according to reporting by The Edge Malaysia citing people familiar with the matter, a person close to President Prabowo asked him to step down. Senior Deputy Governor Destry Damayanti is acting governor and is viewed by insiders as a possible successor; parliament, dominated by the president’s allies, must approve a new appointment. The government says the central bank’s independence is guaranteed by law and that coordination between fiscal and monetary authorities remains necessary.

The market issue is the gap between legal independence and perceived independence. Warjiyo had prioritised monetary and financial stability while the government sought faster growth and easier liquidity. Bank Indonesia raised its benchmark rate three times since May and used defensive measures to support the rupiah, but those actions also increased government borrowing costs. A successor who is formally independent but appointed after a politically pressured resignation may still face a credibility discount. Investors do not need to believe that every decision is politically dictated; they only need to believe that the framework can change when political objectives collide with currency stability.

South Korea’s Bank of Korea offers a less dramatic but instructive reserve-management story. The BOK is resuming physical gold purchases after 13 years, buying export-bound domestic output through the Korea Exchange and the Korea Securities Depository. Gold represented only 1.1% of its US$427.36 billion in foreign-exchange reserves at the end of June, and annual domestic production is about 40–45 tonnes, of which only 4–5 tonnes is exported. The immediate reserve effect will therefore be small. The policy is mainly about creating an option to diversify away from dollar-centred assets without disrupting the local gold market through large open-market orders.

The contrast is useful. Indonesia is testing whether institutional credibility can survive political pressure; Korea is building credibility through a rule-bound, low-impact purchasing channel. In both cases, the state is managing confidence as an economic resource. A central bank can hold the legal power to act, but if households, firms, or investors doubt the rules, the cost appears in currency weakness, funding spreads, or reduced willingness to commit capital.

## 3. Everyday microeconomics: personal data is becoming part of the price tag

US states are moving against retail pricing systems that use individual or behavioural data to set different prices. New Jersey recently became the latest state to limit how grocers can use consumer data to calculate prices, following measures in Maryland and Connecticut; New York lawmakers also approved restrictions that await the governor’s signature. The Federal Trade Commission is considering national action on unfair or deceptive fee practices in online grocery sales. Retailers argue that electronic shelf labels and data-based discounts can improve price accuracy and reduce waste, while critics worry that the same tools can charge more to shoppers who have fewer substitutes or less time to compare.

The economic distinction is between a public promotion and an opaque personal price. A loyalty discount published on stated terms lets consumers understand the exchange. A price inferred from location, shopping history, urgency, or household data is harder to observe and challenge. The buyer sees the final number but not the reference price, the data used, or whether the system has identified a lack of transport or nearby competition as willingness to pay. Disclosure alone is weak here because the information needed to contest the price sits inside the seller’s model.

This is an old problem in a new interface. In 1982, three Cleveland-area supermarket chains paid more than US$4 million in fines and agreed to distribute US$20 million in coupons after a price-fixing case. Today’s concern is less likely to be a visible agreement between managers and more likely to be a shared data or software layer that makes similar prices appear independently chosen. The enforcement question shifts from “Did the firms communicate directly?” to “Did the pricing system reduce independent decision-making or exploit information that consumers could not reasonably access?”

## 4. Tech, AI, and the new political economy: regulation is moving toward the whole value chain

The EU AI Act’s latest implementation changes are now in force. The Digital Omnibus on AI, applicable from 27 July, delays some high-risk obligations while keeping near-term transparency duties: disclosure when people interact with covered AI systems and disclosure of deepfakes in the relevant cases apply from 2 August 2026. The revised dates put watermarking obligations at 2 December 2026, high-risk use cases such as employment, education, credit, critical infrastructure, law enforcement, and border management at 2 December 2027, and certain AI safety components at 2 August 2028.

The delay gives firms more time to build testing and documentation, but it also exposes the difference between notice and remedy. An applicant can be told that AI was used in recruitment without seeing the ranking logic, the data error, or the person responsible for correcting the record. The EU guidance treats worker management broadly, covering recruitment, allocation, monitoring, evaluation, promotion, pay, and termination. That scope matters because AI may substitute for a task while leaving the worker with less control over the decision and no share of the resulting productivity gain.

Australia is watching a similar problem through competition and consumer law rather than a single AI statute. A current legal analysis describes proposed unfair-trading rules aimed at dark patterns, growing concern about agentic AI steering or self-preferencing, and scrutiny of AI partnerships and “acqui-hires” that may remove a young competitor without a conventional merger. The Australian Competition and Consumer Commission has also pursued restrictions on default search-engine preinstallation, with Google agreeing to remove clauses in agreements with Australian telcos that could block emerging search tools, including AI-enhanced competitors.

The supply chain is the point. Market power can sit in model weights, cloud capacity, chips, distribution defaults, data access, or the interface through which a consumer makes a decision. A merger filing may capture one form of concentration, while an exclusive partnership or hiring deal captures another. If regulation only examines the final chatbot or the final price, it misses the upstream arrangements that determine who can enter, what users see, and who captures the surplus.

Across the three themes, this week’s pattern is clear: economic access is being shaped by institutions and software that ordinary users cannot easily inspect. Bangladesh’s migrant workers need enforceable routes to income; Indonesia’s borrowers and investors need a credible monetary framework; grocery shoppers need prices that are not silently personalised against them; and workers need AI decisions that can be challenged. The practical standard is the same in each case: can the affected person understand the rule, stop the harm, and obtain a remedy at a cost below the loss?
