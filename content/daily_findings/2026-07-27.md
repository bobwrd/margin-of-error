# 2026-07-27

The week’s sharpest economic question is who pays for systems built to make allocation more automated. AI data centres are creating pressure over electricity bills, grocery retailers are being pushed to disclose or stop data-based price discrimination, and regulators are filling gaps left by federal retreat on small-dollar credit. The common thread is distribution: headline growth can coexist with higher household costs and weaker ability to challenge decisions.

## 1. Development, justice, and access

Illinois became the second US state to regulate buy-now-pay-later lenders after the Trump administration dropped a Biden-era federal rule. The state law steps into a gap around disclosure, dispute rights, and lender obligations for short-term instalment credit. The policy issue is not whether BNPL is always harmful. It is that a payment product aimed at shoppers who may lack cash or conventional credit can make borrowing look cheaper than it is when fees, missed-payment consequences, and multiple simultaneous plans are hard to see. ([Law.com](https://www.law.com/corpcounsel/2026/07/20/after-trump-administration-scraps-buy-now-pay-later-rule-states-step-in/))

This is a familiar access problem in a new wrapper. A consumer may be included in a digital payment system while remaining exposed to expensive or poorly understood credit. State-by-state rules also raise the cost of compliance for lenders, but that cost is part of the price of making credit legible. Without a common federal floor, protection depends more heavily on where a borrower lives and on whether state regulators have the staff to enforce the rules.

Civil legal access showed the same pattern. North Carolina’s new budget sharply reduces the future role of Interest on Lawyers’ Trust Accounts in funding civil legal aid. The programme provided about $12 million for civil legal services in fiscal year 2025, including nearly $2 million for Pisgah Legal Services; the budget permits only $2 million a year from older IOLTA funds, with restrictions on eligible providers and cases. ([The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/newsletters/2026/07/iolta-north-carolina-legislature-funding-cut/688044/))

The economic loss is not limited to the missing grant money. Civil legal aid helps households contest evictions, recover benefits, resist scams, and obtain protection orders. When representation disappears, a legal right can remain formally available while becoming practically unusable. The result is a transfer from prevention to crisis: more housing instability, unpaid claims, and emergency intervention after a dispute has become harder to fix.

## 2. Everyday microeconomics: the price is increasingly personal

New Jersey’s Fair Price Protection Act bans food retailers and grocery-delivery platforms from using personal or behavioural data to set different prices for individuals or groups. The law covers information such as browsing history, location, income, household details, and other data used to estimate willingness to pay. It preserves ordinary loyalty discounts and does not ban every form of demand-based pricing. New Jersey is the third state this year to target this form of “surveillance pricing,” after Maryland and Connecticut. ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/business-and-practice/new-jersey-bans-food-retailers-from-using-personalized-pricing))

The distinction between dynamic and surveillance pricing matters. A price that rises because a store has low stock or a ride is in unusually high demand is visible, at least in principle, and tied to market conditions. A price tailored from a shopper’s data is harder to contest because the buyer may not know which information was used or whether a similar customer saw a different price. The concern is strongest for necessities, where urgency and limited alternatives make willingness to pay a poor measure of genuine choice.

The New Jersey law also pauses the installation of new electronic shelf labels for one year and allows enforcement through consumer-fraud rules. That shows how a small retail technology can become a governance issue. Electronic labels reduce the cost of changing prices, while personal data can make those changes more targeted. Together they move firms closer to continuous price experiments. The policy response is therefore aimed at the information advantage, not at every change in price.

## 3. Tech, infrastructure, and the new political economy

AI infrastructure is now creating a direct fight over who bears the cost of growth. The Trump administration is expanding a voluntary pledge involving governors and electricity companies that is meant to protect households from higher utility bills caused by data-centre demand. The political problem is straightforward: data centres can bring construction, tax revenue, and national strategic value, but grid upgrades and new generation can be paid for through rates that reach households that receive none of the technology’s profits. ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/trump-ai-data-centers-pledge-490ea7e4c7227d5e550b00a0056c33c9))

A voluntary pledge does not settle the allocation question. Regulators still need to decide how much new capacity a project should fund, whether large users receive subsidies, and how costs are assigned when a facility is built for one company but the network investment serves a wider region. If the answer is unclear, “AI growth” can become a mechanism for socialising infrastructure costs while keeping the returns concentrated among model providers, chip firms, and data-centre owners.

The financing chain is becoming just as concentrated. Nvidia said it would acquire $1 billion of newly issued shares in South Korea’s Naver as part of a partnership to build a data centre. Reuters also reported that Nvidia was in talks to provide roughly $250 billion of financing support for an OpenAI data-centre project. The figures are reported plans or discussions, not completed investment, but they show how chip supply, model demand, capital, and infrastructure are being tied together inside a small group of firms. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/nvidia-acquire-1-billion-new-shares-south-koreas-naver-2026-07-26/); [Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/global-markets-wrapup-1-2026-07-27/))

That structure can speed deployment while making competition harder. A firm that supplies the scarce chips, finances the data centre, and has a strategic stake in the model customer is not operating in a simple supplier market. The relevant question for regulators is not only whether AI services become cheaper at the interface. It is whether control over the inputs lets a few firms capture the surplus and shape which competitors can reach the market.

## 4. AI, labour, and proof of harm

A lawsuit by Meta workers illustrates the gap between an automated workplace decision and a legally provable claim. The workers allege that AI-based layoff selection relied on productivity and AI-token measures that disadvantaged employees who had taken medical or family leave. A US judge declined to block the layoffs while the claims proceed, and the workers face private arbitration. Reuters reported that their lawyers have struggled to obtain evidence showing how the systems were used. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/world-at-work/meta-employees-lawsuit-shows-that-if-ai-fires-you-proving-it-is-hard-part-2026-07-22/))

The mechanism is not new: a performance metric can reward uninterrupted availability and penalise workers whose time is interrupted by illness or care. The new problem is opacity. If the employer controls the training data, feature definitions, thresholds, and records of human overrides, the worker must prove discrimination without access to the material needed to test the employer’s explanation. Arbitration can further reduce collective discovery and public scrutiny. A productivity system may therefore shift risk to workers before courts have a realistic way to inspect it.

The broader distribution problem is visible in current AI markets. China’s Kimi K3 halted new subscriptions after demand overwhelmed its capacity, while cheaper Chinese models have put pressure on US technology shares and raised questions about the pricing power of US AI firms. ([AP](https://apnews.com/article/kimi-k3-china-ai-model-us-4c66a2e0f557ce79d3cc2d769c9a6226))

Lower model prices could benefit users, but cheap access is not the same as broad economic gains. The firms that control chips, electricity access, cloud capacity, and distribution can still capture much of the value even when the final product is inexpensive. For workers, the test is whether AI complements tasks and raises bargaining power, or whether it becomes a measurement and surveillance layer that makes employment more contingent while leaving the gains with the platform.

This week’s pattern across development, everyday markets, and technology is a contest over hidden transfers. BNPL can widen access while shifting risk to borrowers, personalised pricing can make ordinary shopping depend on private data, and AI infrastructure can raise public electricity costs while returns accrue to a narrow supply chain. The practical response is to make allocation rules visible, give affected people usable appeal routes, and require large firms to carry a clearer share of the costs created by their growth.
