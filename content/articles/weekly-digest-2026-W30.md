Oil rose above $100 a barrel this week as conflict disrupted shipping through the Gulf. At the same time, investors began questioning whether the AI buildout can earn back its infrastructure bill: Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle could spend $534 billion more on capital expenditure in 2027 than in 2025, against about $340 billion in additional operating cash flow. These look like separate stories. They are linked by who has room to absorb the adjustment.

## Access can fail below the account level

Reuters reported that two Israeli banks are preparing to end correspondent relationships with Palestinian banks in September and October. The affected households and firms may still have bank accounts, but without a dependable payment corridor those accounts cannot reliably support wages, trade, or remittances. The substitutes are cash and informal intermediaries, which cost more and offer less protection when a payment is frozen or misdirected.

The same gap appears in legal access. Former Meta employees suing over alleged discriminatory AI-assisted layoffs say the hard part is proving how the tools were used. The employer controls the model and the records. A worker knows the outcome but may not have the evidence to challenge it. A right that cannot be investigated is a weak right.

## A fuel shock becomes a credit shock

Rerouting oil tankers around the Cape of Good Hope can add 48 days and roughly $2.5 million to a voyage. Shipping firms may hedge or negotiate. Households cannot do either with the fuel, food, and electricity bills that follow. Lower-income families spend more of their income on necessities and have less cash to wait.

That is where credit enters. A household may put a higher energy bill on a card, use buy-now-pay-later for groceries, or take a digital loan. Each decision can look manageable. Together they leave no buffer. Illinois became the second U.S. state to regulate BNPL lenders after federal rules were abandoned, leaving borrowers to navigate different protections for the same checkout product. Financial inclusion should be measured by whether people can see total obligations, dispute an error, and pause repayment during a shock, not by approval rates alone.

## AI is expensive before it is productive

Big Tech’s spending plans are becoming a market-power question. Cloud firms can spread data-centre costs through service prices and long contracts. Smaller companies cannot negotiate on equal terms when a few firms control compute, distribution, and the data needed to improve models. Moonshot AI’s pause on new Kimi subscriptions after demand strained capacity made the physical bottleneck visible: an open-weight model still needs scarce chips, servers, electricity, and network capacity.

The labour effects are uneven. The ILO estimates that 22.9% of ASEAN employment, nearly 80 million workers, has more than minimal exposure to generative AI. Exposure does not equal job loss. It can mean faster work, tighter monitoring, fewer junior tasks, or more cases assigned to the same employee. Output may rise while bargaining power falls. The gain reaches workers through higher pay, training, or a say over how jobs change.

Next week, watch the pass-through. Will higher shipping and energy costs reach food and transport prices? Will regulators require records and disclosure when AI affects employment? And will AI infrastructure remain a private toll road, or will policy make compute, data, and alternative platforms usable for smaller firms and ordinary workers? The dividing line is simple: who can wait, switch, appeal, or borrow without turning a short shock into a long loss?
