The week’s most useful economic signal was a split-screen one: US payrolls fell by 23,000 in July while investors continued to price large gains from AI infrastructure. The unemployment rate held at 4.1%, but labour-force participation slipped to 61.4% and earlier job growth was revised down by 103,000. The question is not whether technology raises output. It is who gets the gain before the adjustment bill arrives.

## A weak hiring market changes the AI bargain

The labour data does not prove that AI caused July’s weakness. It does show why aggregate productivity is incomplete. Financial-activities employment fell by 14,000 in July and is down 121,000 from its May 2025 peak, while health care added 22,000 jobs, below its recent average. If firms invest in software while cutting entry-level hiring, output per worker can rise even as new workers lose the first rung into the sector.

The distribution depends on task design and bargaining power. Automation can remove routine processing and create more demand for compliance, sales, or risk work. It can also let a firm keep output steady with fewer people. Workers receive the gain only when they can move into complementary tasks, negotiate pay, or obtain training that is useful outside one employer.

## “Free” digital payments still have an incidence

India’s UPI processed a record 23.66 billion transactions worth ₹29.88 lakh crore in July. Parliament is considering a framework that could permit merchant charges on selected transactions, with reports pointing to fees of roughly 0.3%–0.5% for higher-value payments by larger merchants. A targeted fee may fund fraud controls and settlement without taxing small-ticket users. A broad fee could push merchants toward cash or another wallet.

The legal payer and economic payer are not always the same. A large retailer may absorb the cost; a smaller seller may raise prices, split transactions, or steer customers toward a platform with less visible terms. Policymakers should publish the rail’s cost base and track failed transactions, merchant exit, complaints, and price pass-through. Inclusion is not proved by volume if the service becomes harder to use when a dispute occurs.

## Courts and subscriptions expose the price of friction

India’s e-Courts project has digitised more than 753 crore pages, processed 1.25 crore e-filings, and supported over 4.18 crore remote hearings. Those tools cut travel and filing costs. They do not replace representation. A litigant still needs help understanding evidence, procedure, and the consequences of an error. In the US, a lapsed legal-services contract disrupted representation for unaccompanied children while more than three million immigration cases remained pending.

The FTC’s case against Hims & Hers shows the household version. The agency alleges that users were charged after an intake flow, enrolled in recurring treatment, exposed to advertising-related data sharing, and made to navigate a difficult cancellation process. The allegations remain unresolved. The mechanism is familiar: friction at exit turns status quo bias into revenue, while the consumer must preserve records and pursue a remedy.

## Data, AI, and the next distribution fight

Meta is asking a California judge to block Mark Zuckerberg’s deposition in copyright suits over AI training practices. The procedural dispute points to a larger question: when books, posts, images, and human review become inputs to a model, who captures the surplus? The same issue applies to workers who label data, verify outputs, or absorb the risk of automated decisions. A productivity claim that ignores ownership and bargaining power is only half an economic account.

Next week, watch whether weak hiring changes the political case for AI support, whether India’s payment-fee design protects small merchants, and whether courts turn digital access into usable representation. Across these stories, the test is practical: can the affected person see the rule, stop the harm, and appeal without spending more than the loss is worth?
