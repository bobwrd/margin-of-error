The Monetary Authority of Singapore said AI-related firms account for about 40% of S&P 500 market capitalisation, more than 30% of emerging-markets Asia, and 87% of new venture-capital funding. In the same week, the European Commission backed seven AI gigafactories and the US Federal Trade Commission sued Hims & Hers over alleged privacy, billing, and cancellation practices. The stories look different, but they ask the same question: who carries the cost when digital systems scale faster than the protections around them?

## The AI boom has a household transmission channel

MAS warned that model builders and hyperscalers may need debt and equity financing well beyond current commercial revenue. If the investment cycle works, productivity may raise output. If it fails, the reversal will travel through data-centre orders, chip demand, lenders, contractors, utility rates, and asset prices. Households and smaller firms have less ability to hedge or refinance than the companies controlling compute and capital.

That is why AI infrastructure belongs in financial-stability policy. A productivity gain is not a distributional result. It can become lower staffing, faster targets, or a larger margin. Workers receive the gain only if bargaining power, training, and access to complementary tasks move with the technology.

## Access is more than being allowed to sign up

The FTC’s Hims & Hers complaint alleges that sensitive health information was shared with advertising platforms, consumers were charged soon after submitting intake forms, recurring prescriptions were created without a meaningful review, and cancellation was difficult. The allegations remain for the court to decide. The economic mechanism is already clear: the provider controls the interface and the records, while the consumer must detect the charge, preserve evidence, and pursue a remedy.

Singapore’s Money Lock function shows the better direction. About S$47 billion was protected from digital transfers as of May, and reported scam cases and losses fell in 2025. Changing the default transaction path can prevent harm before a complaint is needed. But people with unstable incomes or weak digital access may still accept expensive informal credit because they need cash immediately. Financial inclusion should be measured by safe options and successful remedies, not account totals.

## The price can be personal without looking different

New Jersey’s Fair Price Protection Act bars grocery retailers and delivery platforms from using personal or behavioural data to set different prices for individuals or groups. It preserves ordinary loyalty discounts and distinguishes them from a price calculated from what a seller infers about one shopper’s urgency, location, income, or household.

For necessities, willingness to pay is a poor measure of preference. A family may pay more because it lacks transport, time, or a nearby alternative. The buyer usually cannot see the comparison price or the data that produced the offer, so disclosure alone does little. The same logic appears in subscriptions: a quick sign-up followed by immediate billing and a difficult exit turns inattention into revenue. An exit path as easy as entry is a more useful rule than another long privacy notice.

## Public money should buy usable access

The EU’s plan to mobilise more than €30 billion around seven AI gigafactories responds to the concentration of chips, electricity, cloud capacity, and data-centre finance. Public support may widen access, but it may also protect incumbents if smaller firms cannot use the infrastructure or if taxpayers absorb early losses while private firms keep the upside. The EU AI Act’s logging, documentation, human-oversight, and risk-management rules matter for the same reason: an affected worker, borrower, or applicant needs a way to identify the rule, challenge an error, and obtain compensation.

Next week, watch whether higher bond yields and AI financing concerns reach utility bills, hiring plans, and smaller businesses. Also watch how regulators turn complaint rights into actual recoveries. The test across all three markets is simple: can the person carrying the downside see the rule, stop the transaction, and appeal without spending more than the loss is worth?
