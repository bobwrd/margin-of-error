The European Commission published its guidelines on AI Act transparency obligations on 20 July 2026, less than two weeks before most Article 50 duties begin to apply. The guidance covers when people must be told they are interacting with AI and how providers and deployers must mark or label synthetic content.

## What the guidance covers

Article 50 is aimed at deception risks rather than model capability. Providers of interactive AI systems must design them to inform people when they are directly interacting with a machine, unless that fact is obvious from the context. Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video, or text must add machine-readable marks so the artificial origin can be detected.

Deployers have separate duties. They must disclose deepfakes, AI-generated or manipulated image, audio, or video content that resembles existing people, places, or events. They must also disclose AI-generated text on matters of public interest where the text is published to inform the public, unless it has undergone substantive human review or editorial control. Other disclosures apply to emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems in the situations covered by the Act.

The Commission's Q&A and guidelines are not a new regulation. They explain how the Commission reads the existing provision. The binding baseline remains Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and its Article 50 text.

## The dates are the point

The main transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026. The Commission says systems already on the market before that date have until 2 December 2026 to comply with the marking obligations for AI-generated content. That transition is narrow. It does not turn the whole Article 50 regime into a voluntary exercise, and it does not remove duties that apply to newly placed systems or to deployers.

The timing leaves little room for companies to treat this as a later policy project. Product teams need to know which outputs are synthetic, whether a human review qualifies as substantive, where machine-readable signals survive distribution, and how users will see a disclosure without it being stripped during editing or reposting.

## Why this matters

The rule reaches across the AI supply chain. A model provider may need to build provenance or marking into the system. A platform or publisher may become the deployer responsible for disclosure when it presents or republishes content. An organisation using an emotion-recognition tool has a different duty from a company operating a chatbot, but both need a reliable way to identify when Article 50 applies.

That makes the compliance problem operational. A policy document alone will not show that a label was present, that an exception was valid, or that a content pipeline preserved a machine-readable mark. Teams will need inventories, product controls, review records, and tests using real distribution paths.

The wider AI Act context is mixed. The Commission's governance page places the AI Office and national market-surveillance authorities in charge of implementation and enforcement. The Digital Omnibus has extended some high-risk timelines, including rules for certain systems used in regulated products. Transparency is on a different clock. The delay for selected high-risk obligations should not be read as a delay for Article 50's core disclosure duties.

The European Parliament's Digital Omnibus briefing shows why the timeline remains politically contested: lawmakers are trying to reduce implementation friction while keeping safeguards around content, rights, and high-impact uses. DLA Piper's comparative analysis places the EU's approach beside other national regimes, but it is commentary, not binding law. Future of Life Institute's work on AI Act enforcement likewise helps frame the institutional problem but does not replace the Commission's text.

## Enforcement implications

Article 99 allows administrative fines for breaches of the Act. For an undertaking, the upper limit can reach €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, subject to the Act's proportionality rules. The size of the maximum is less important than the evidence trail behind a product decision. A company that can show a clear scope assessment, working disclosure controls, and a reasoned exception is in a better position than one that simply says it intended to comply.

The immediate test is simple: when someone in the EU encounters AI-generated content or an AI interaction after 2 August, can the responsible provider or deployer explain what the person was told, how the signal was added, and why any exception applied? The Commission has now given the market its answer on the main cases. The deadline is next.
