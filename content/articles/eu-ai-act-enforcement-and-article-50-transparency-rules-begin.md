The EU AI Act entered its enforcement phase on 2 August 2026, and the same date brought Article 50’s transparency duties into operation. The European Commission’s AI Office and national authorities now have responsibility for applying the Act, while people in the EU must increasingly be told when they are dealing with AI or seeing content made or altered by it.

## What changed on 2 August

Article 50 covers four practical situations. Providers of systems designed to interact directly with people must disclose that the interaction is with AI, unless that fact is obvious in context. Providers of systems that generate synthetic audio, images, video, or text must use machine-readable marks so the outputs can be detected as artificial where technically feasible.

Deployers have separate duties. They must inform people exposed to emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems. They must disclose deepfakes, and they must label AI-generated or manipulated text published to inform the public about matters of public interest, unless the material went through human review and editorial control under a responsible publisher.

The requirements are broader than the high-risk part of the Act. A consumer chatbot, synthetic-media workflow, newsroom tool, or marketing system does not need to be classified as high risk to trigger an Article 50 obligation. That is why the date matters to far more organisations than the delayed high-risk timetable.

## Enforcement is now a real operating function

The Commission’s AI Office supervises general-purpose AI models and works with national market-surveillance authorities. Those authorities enforce rules for AI systems, including transparency requirements and prohibited practices. The Commission has also published a complaints tool and a whistleblower channel, giving outside parties routes into the enforcement system.

The first compliance question is not whether a company signed the Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content. The code is voluntary. The legal duties are not. Signatories get a recognised framework for showing how they meet marking and labelling duties. Organisations that use another approach must be ready to show that their measures are equally adequate when an authority examines them.

That distinction puts pressure on evidence. Companies need an inventory of AI systems, a map of where outputs are published, records of vendor responsibilities, and a way to test whether labels survive ordinary distribution. A notice that appears in a generation interface but disappears when content is exported or reposted may not do the job the rule requires.

## Why the rule matters

Article 50 moves AI transparency from a product claim to a point-of-exposure duty. The relevant question is what a person sees or interacts with, not only what a model provider says about its system. Publishers and platforms therefore sit inside the compliance chain even when they did not train the underlying model.

The economic effect will arrive through product changes rather than one large licensing bill. Firms will modify interfaces, add provenance and labelling systems, update editorial policies, renegotiate contracts, and document exceptions. Smaller organisations may rely on vendor controls, but that shifts the need for assurance into procurement and monitoring.

The wider policy choice is clear. The EU has delayed some high-risk obligations to give regulators and firms more time, but it has not postponed the basic information layer. Bruegel’s recent analysis argues that AI rules need a better balance between requirements imposed before deployment and monitoring after deployment. Article 50 is a modest version of that second concern: it does not decide whether a system is safe, but it gives users and authorities a clearer signal about what they are seeing.

## Global context

The EU is not the only jurisdiction moving toward provenance and disclosure rules. But Article 50 combines direct interaction notices, machine-readable marking, deepfake labels, and public-interest text rules in one cross-border framework. Companies serving multiple markets may build to the EU baseline, while others will keep separate controls for each jurisdiction.

The next test is enforcement consistency. The Commission has supplied guidance and a code, but national authorities will still face edge cases involving satire, editing, human review, reposting, and technical limits. The first investigations will show whether Article 50 becomes a predictable operational standard or another compliance obligation whose meaning is settled only after disputes.
