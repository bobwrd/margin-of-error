Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2026/1755 was adopted on 20 July 2026 and published in the Official Journal on 21 July 2026. It supplies the operating rules for two powers that were already in the AI Act: Commission evaluations of general-purpose AI models under Article 92 and fine proceedings against their providers under Article 101.

## What changed

The regulation does not create the AI Office's authority from scratch. The authority comes from Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. Article 92 lets the Commission conduct evaluations, appoint independent experts, and request access to a general-purpose model, including through APIs or source code. Article 101 allows fines of up to 3% of worldwide annual turnover or €15 million, whichever is higher, where a provider intentionally or negligently breaches the GPAI rules or fails to provide required access or information.

The new text tells the Commission how to use those powers. An access decision must identify the technical means, tools, components, conditions, and time limit. Depending on the evaluation, access may include an API, internal systems, source code, model weights, hosting infrastructure, or the ability to inspect and modify system state during an interaction. The provider cannot impose technical constraints that materially frustrate a proper evaluation. The Commission can also require logging to be disabled where that is needed to protect the integrity and confidentiality of the work.

That is a serious request, but it is not unlimited. The regulation says access must be appropriate to the evaluation's objective. Independent experts must meet independence and security requirements, make declarations of interest, and protect confidential information.

## The enforcement sequence

The regulation separates investigation from a formal fine proceeding. Before opening proceedings, the Commission may investigate and request measures. In urgent cases, it may order interim measures where there is a prima facie infringement and a risk of serious damage to health, safety, or another public interest covered by the AI Act. One possible measure is preventing a general-purpose model from being made available on the EU market.

If the Commission moves toward a fine, it must notify preliminary findings and give the provider a chance to respond. The provider's written observations must have at least 21 days. The provider receives non-confidential versions of documents cited in the findings, while the Commission must balance access to the file against business secrecy and other confidential information. The regulation also adds limitation periods for imposing and enforcing fines.

The scope matters. This procedure is aimed at providers of general-purpose AI models. It is not a general enforcement manual for every high-risk AI system or every company that deploys an AI tool. A downstream organisation may still face duties under other parts of the Act, but this particular Commission procedure targets the model layer.

## Why providers should care

The practical shift is from a legal power on paper to a repeatable supervisory process. Model providers now have to plan for requests that reach below a public API and into the systems used to train, host, test, and monitor a model. They also have to preserve a defensible record of cooperation, access controls, safety testing, and the reasons for any refusal or limitation.

The regulation's safeguards matter too. A provider has a defined opportunity to answer preliminary findings, challenge the scope of disclosure, and protect trade secrets. Those safeguards do not remove the exposure. They make the process more predictable, which can make enforcement easier to run and harder to dismiss as improvised.

The Commission's governance page places the AI Office at the centre of supervision for the most capable models, while national authorities handle much of the wider Act. The European Parliament's Digital Omnibus briefing describes the parallel political effort to simplify implementation and shift some high-risk dates. The two tracks should not be confused: delayed high-risk obligations do not erase the GPAI enforcement machinery.

Future of Life Institute welcomed the procedural framework while arguing that its independence, access, and accountability rules need careful scrutiny. DLA Piper's comparative guide treats the EU's fine structure as part of a wider regulatory pattern, but its cross-jurisdictional commentary is analysis, not binding interpretation. The binding text is the Official Journal regulation and the AI Act itself.

The result is a clearer escalation path: structured dialogue, technical access, preliminary findings, a response period, and a possible fine or interim market measure. For GPAI providers, the enforcement question is no longer only whether the Act applies. It is whether the company can withstand a regulator looking inside the model's operating reality.
